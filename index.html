Use tendon biology to inform your reasoning, not to memorise a protocol. Explore the tendon, then run the load lab and see what makes it adapt, settle or flare.

Where to go

By the end you can

  1. Explain tendon function and adaptation in language useful for clinical decisions.
  2. Recognise a likely tendinopathy and know when to think again or refer.
  3. Identify what raised demand or lowered capacity.
  4. Explain tendinopathy without reinforcing fear or fragility.
  5. Prescribe and progress force, rate and energy-storage loading for the person and task.
  6. Use symptom and next-day response to adjust load, without treating pain as a damage meter.
  7. Build a return-to-activity plan linking goal, demand, capacity and exercise.

Three things to take away

  1. Rest calms a sore tendon but does not make it stronger. The right loading does.
  2. Some soreness during and after exercise is normal and safe. How it settles by the next morning is the guide.
  3. A scan shows structure. It does not show how strong the tendon is or how much it should hurt.

The loading toolbox

Dose the adaptation

Exercise becomes rehabilitation when the stimulus is measurable and progressive.

  • Load: resistance, sets and reps, effort (RPE or reps in reserve), range, tempo, frequency.
  • Progress by: more load, more range, higher speed, more volume, less assistance, more task specificity.
Programming challenge. Jane does 3 × 15 hip abduction at RPE 3/10 for four weeks. Symptoms are stable but strength and function have not changed. The stimulus is too light to drive adaptation: raise the load before adding more exercises.

If it flares

  1. What changed? Sport volume, a new exercise, more range, less recovery, a work spike.
  2. How big is the response? Symptoms, morning stiffness, function, how long it lasts.
  3. What can we modify? Reduce the most provocative dose for now.
  4. What can stay? Keep tolerable strength work and general activity.
  5. How do we rebuild? Progress again once it settles and the driver is understood.

A flare is a signal to adjust the plan. It is not proof of reinjury and it is not back to square one.

PACE tendon capacity framework

  1. Goal: what do they want to return to?
  2. Required load: what must the tendon and person tolerate?
  3. Current capacity: what can they tolerate today?
  4. Gap: where are the important mismatches?
  5. Modify: which provocative loads need temporary adjustment?
  6. Build: what stimulus develops the missing capacity?
  7. Expose: how do we return to the meaningful task?
  8. Monitor: how did symptoms and function respond during, later and next day?

When the story does not fit

Sudden traumatic loss of function, a felt "pop" with weakness, unexpected neurological symptoms, systemic features, severe unexplained or night pain, or failure to progress as expected: reconsider the diagnosis and refer.

Also consider bursitis or adjacent joint pathology, bone stress injury, referred or radicular pain, and inflammatory or systemic disease. Ask about recent fluoroquinolone antibiotics and corticosteroid use.

Six Monday-morning rules

  1. Treat the person, not the scan.
  2. Understand the load–capacity gap.
  3. Rest reduces load. It does not build capacity.
  4. Isometrics and eccentrics are tools, not magic treatments.
  5. Progress force, then range, rate and task demands as required.
  6. Monitor response and progress by function, not a rigid calendar.
